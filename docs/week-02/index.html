Why integrate the forecast?

The Week 1 workbook already projected THX-101 sales from management assumptions about prescription growth and pricing. Building another isolated series would show which model fits the data, but it wouldn't tell us what happens to the business when that forecast changes.

I kept the original assumptions as the default and added a forecast-method selector in Revenue & Demand (cell O6). It offers management projections alongside three simple moving averages, a three-month exponential moving average, ARIMA with drift, and a seasonal ARIMA alternative. The separate time-series worksheet was removed.

The forecast controls affect future months only. That preserves our modeled historical record, avoids rewriting the previous statements, and gives us a direct way to compare statistical projections with the original business plan.

Data and evaluation

I used 33 months of synthetic THX-101 product sales from January 2024 through September 2026. Each method was tested using a fixed forecast origin: the first 27 months (through March 2026) were used for training, while April–September 2026 was withheld for a six-month out-of-sample comparison. The forecast horizon runs from October 2026 through September 2027.

For a fair comparison, I evaluated all methods against those same six withheld observations using mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) and root mean squared error (RMSE). MAPE gives the proportional forecasting error; RMSE expresses error in millions of dollars and penalizes larger misses.

Model selection: what performed best?

ARIMA(0,1,0) with drift recorded the lowest six-month holdout error, 3.15% MAPE (RMSE $0.344 million). Its advantage makes sense: monthly sales increased steadily, so a model that extends the average historical monthly change tracks this dataset better than a moving average that lags behind the rising level.

The twelve-month moving average performed particularly poorly (30.34% MAPE) because it averages months with much lower sales and cannot anticipate that the commercialization ramp will continue. That's a limitation of the chosen forecast method for an increasing trend, not proof that moving averages are generally unreliable.

Stationarity and seasonality

The level series trends upward. An earlier Augmented Dickey–Fuller diagnostic returned a level-series p-value near 0.945; after first differencing, the p-value was approximately 0.057, still slightly above the conventional 5% threshold. I therefore treated first differencing as a practical specification and reviewed ACF/PACF diagnostics on the monthly changes.

The differenced series showed a lag-12 correlation close to zero, so there isn't strong independent evidence of a repeating annual pattern. I tested a seasonal alternative anyway, using a lag-12 relationship fitted to the differenced observations. Its 3.58% MAPE was close to ARIMA's, but did not improve the six-month test result. With fewer than three annual cycles, I would not treat that as a reliable seasonal effect.

Business interpretation and limitations

For September 2027, the ARIMA model projects approximately $13.26 million in monthly THX-101 sales, compared with $12.92 million under our original management assumptions. The difference is modest, but it creates a measurable alternative for prescription demand, production planning, royalties, and financing needs.

I tested switching the workbook from Management to ARIMA and confirmed that future sales and downstream operating calculations changed while the checked balance-sheet and cash-flow reconciliations remained at zero. I restored Management as the default after testing.

The main limitation is that the history itself is synthetic and follows assumptions we originally designed. ARIMA's 3.15% is a six-month holdout MAPE on that constructed series—not a claim of 96.85% real-world accuracy, nor a validated error rate for future sales. Regulatory outcomes, reimbursement, supply constraints, and competition can change revenue in ways that an extrapolation cannot predict.

Implementation note: the selectable forecasts use native spreadsheet formulas, not VBA. The nonseasonal model applies first differences and historical drift; the seasonal alternative uses a conditional least-squares lag-12 relationship rather than a full iterative maximum-likelihood fit. ADF p-values were retained from the earlier diagnostic run and are not live spreadsheet recalculations.
