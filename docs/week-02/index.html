Objective

I used monthly THX-101 product sales from the existing TitanHelix financial model to test which forecasting methods best captured the company's rapid commercialization. The data is entirely synthetic, based on the Week 1 workbook—not real-world observed drug sales.

Each model was trained on January 2024 through March 2026, then tested against six months of withheld sales (April–September 2026). After comparing the holdout errors, I produced forecasts for October 2026 through September 2027.

What the model comparison tells us

The moving averages consistently lag the growth in THX-101 sales. A shorter three-month window responds faster than six- or twelve-month windows, but its forecast error is still substantially higher than ARIMA's.

The nonseasonal ARIMA (0,1,0) with drift produced the most accurate six-month holdout forecast at 3.15% MAPE, versus 8.25% for the tested seasonal ARIMA specification. Adding an annual seasonal term did not improve this dataset's forecast accuracy.

Limitations and Business Use

With only 33 months of synthetic history, there is limited evidence of a stable annual seasonal pattern. A statistical trend also cannot anticipate changes in regulatory approvals, insurance coverage, supply capacity, or competitive conditions.

The practical value is as a benchmark against the original Week 1 revenue assumptions. TitanHelix could use the differences to examine inventory requirements, licensing royalties, manufacturing capacity, and funding needs without treating one forecast as a guaranteed outcome.
